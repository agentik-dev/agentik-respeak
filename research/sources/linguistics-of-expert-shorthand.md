# The Linguistics of Expert Shorthand: Theory Base for Swarm Dialect Convergence with a Human in the Loop

Research dimension for the **respeak** project. Covers: collaborative reference (Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs), lexical entrainment (Brennan & Clark), interactive alignment (Pickering & Garrod), common ground/grounding (Clark), Zipf's least effort & brevity law, Piantadosi et al. 2011, experimental semiotics (Galantucci; Garrod et al.), and codified expert shorthands as governance case studies (tactical brevity codes, ICAO phraseology, Q codes, maritime signals, medical abbreviations & the Joint Commission "Do Not Use" list, stenographic brief theory, trading hand signals). Ends with transferable design principles.

Date: 2026-08-31

## 1. Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs (1986): Referring as a Collaborative Process

**The tangram experiments.** Pairs of participants, separated by a barrier, repeatedly arranged sets of 12 abstract tangram figures. A "director" had to get a "matcher" to identify each figure, over six trials with the same figures. Because tangrams have no conventional names, pairs had to invent referring expressions from scratch.

**Core findings:**
- **Referring expressions shorten dramatically over trials.** Canonical example (as reproduced in the Wikipedia "Grounding in communication" article): trial 1: "the next one looks like a person who's ice skating, except they're sticking out two arms in front" → trial 4: "The next one's the ice skater" → trial 6: "The ice skater." Word counts drop steeply (roughly from ~40 words to ~1-2 words per figure across trials in Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs' data), and matching gets faster and stays accurate.
- **Reference is collaborative, not unilateral.** The literary model (speaker crafts a perfect description alone) is wrong; instead, speakers present a *provisional* description and partners jointly **refashion** it — via repairs, expansions, and replacements — until both accept it. Example from the paper: A: "Um, third one is the guy reading with, holding his book to the left" / B: "Okay, kind of standing up?" / A: "Yeah." / B: "Okay."
- **Acceptance is required.** A reference is only "grounded" when the addressee gives evidence of understanding (acknowledgment, relevant next action). Presentation + acceptance = a *contribution* to conversation (Clark & Schaefer 1989).
- **Conceptual pacts** (term formalized later by Brennan & Clark 1996): a jointly ratified conceptualization of a referent — not just a shared word, but a shared *perspective* ("the ice skater" implies both partners now see the tangram *as* an ice skater). Pacts are **partner-specific**: when the partner changes, speakers revert to longer descriptions; the shorthand does not automatically transfer. (Wikipedia has no standalone "Conceptual pact" article — the seed URL 404s; the concept is documented under "Lexical entrainment" and "Grounding in communication.")

**Principle of least collaborative effort.** Participants minimize the *total joint* effort across presentation and acceptance phases — not the speaker's effort alone. "The ideal utterances are informative and brief." Two mechanisms visible in the tangram data: (a) refashioning is cheaper than crafting a perfect description on the first try; (b) partners exploit accumulated common ground to shorten expressions and total conversation length. Crucially, the theory predicts parties will *spend more effort* — i.e., decompress — under three conditions (per the grounding literature):
- **Time pressure:** mutual understanding must occur within a deadline.
- **Errors:** when the chance of error is high, or previous low-effort messages caused errors.
- **Ignorance:** when a lack of shared knowledge is notable (e.g., a new partner).

**Respeak mapping:** the compression curve is natural and desirable *between agents that share history*; but the theory itself says compression must be rolled back for any partner lacking the common ground — and the human reader is exactly that partner, permanently. Least *collaborative* effort counts the reader's decoding effort in the total; shorthand that saves the writer 50 tokens but costs the human minutes is a net loss under the actual principle.

## 2. Lexical Entrainment, Interactive Alignment, and Common Ground

### Brennan & Clark (1996): lexical entrainment and conceptual pacts
- **Lexical entrainment** = "the phenomenon by which a speaker adopts the referential terms used by their interlocutor" (Wikipedia). Partners converge on the same term for a referent and stick with it — even when the pact term violates Grice's maxim of quantity (they'll keep saying "the dog" when "the husky" or even "the animal" would do, because the entrained term is jointly ratified and lexically available).
- Two cooperative mechanisms of entrainment (per Wikipedia): **embedded corrections** (using a different term in context without flagging the change) and **exposed corrections** (explicitly negotiating: "by 'girl', do you mean 'Jane'?"). Respeak's lexicon-ratification flow is essentially institutionalized exposed correction.
- Brennan & Clark's key claims: entrainment is **partner-specific** (a pact made with partner A is not assumed with partner B) and **historical** (pacts persist across the conversation once made; breaking a pact without flagging it causes processing cost for the partner).
- LLM relevance, straight from the Wikipedia article: "There is now evidence emerging that LLMs such as GPT-4 demonstrate alignment capabilities similar to that of humans" (Wang, Theune & Srivastava 2024, LNCS 14524) — i.e., agent swarms *will* entrain spontaneously; the design question is governance, not whether it happens.

### Pickering & Garrod (2004): interactive alignment model
(Wikipedia has no "Interactive alignment" article; the model is summarized in the criticisms section of "Grounding in communication" and in the primary literature: Pickering & Garrod, "Toward a mechanistic psychology of dialogue," Behavioral and Brain Sciences 27, 2004.)
- Dialogue succeeds via largely **automatic priming at every linguistic level** — lexical, syntactic, semantic, situation-model. Aligning at one level strengthens alignment at others (percolation). No explicit negotiation or elaborate partner modeling is needed for most alignment; it is a resource-cheap mechanistic process, contrasted with Clark's more deliberate grounding.
- Alignment "enables interlocutors to develop and use **routine expressions**" — semi-fixed phrases that shortcut production and comprehension; and supports "a system for monitoring language processing." Routinization is the mechanism by which a dialogue-local shorthand crystallizes.
- Garrod & Anderson (1987) "maze game" study (precursor): pairs converged on shared description schemes (e.g., coordinate vs. path descriptions of maze positions) without ever discussing them — **implicit convention formation**, via the "output/input coordination" principle: speakers reuse the representations they just comprehended.
- Respeak mapping: alignment is *implicit and inevitable* among homogeneous agents (same model, same context = maximal priming); an unmonitored swarm dialect will drift by routinization without any explicit decisions being made. This is the argument for forcing conventions through an explicit ratification gate: convert Pickering-Garrod implicit drift into Clark-style negotiated pacts that leave an audit trail.

### Clark & Brennan (1991): grounding and its costs
- **Common ground** = the accumulated "mutual knowledge, mutual beliefs, and mutual assumptions" required for communication; **grounding** is the moment-by-moment process of updating it. **Grounding criterion:** "the mutual belief between conversational partners that everyone involved has a clear enough understanding of the concept to move forward" — understanding is grounded *to a criterion sufficient for current purposes*, not absolutely.
- Evidence of understanding: **acknowledgments** (backchannels), **relevant next turn**, **continued attention**. For agents: the "relevant next action" (correct execution) is the strongest cheap evidence; respeak translators can use downstream task success as grounding evidence for shorthand comprehension.
- **Anticipating what a partner knows — community co-membership:** "Members of a group with knowledge in a particular field could use technical jargon when communicating within the group, whereas communicating outside of the group would require them to use layman terms." This is the exact theoretical license for a two-register system: swarm register inside the community, translated register outside it.
- **Media constraints on grounding** (8 factors): copresence, visibility, audibility, contemporality, simultaneity, sequentiality, **reviewability**, **revisability**. Text-based agent communication is weak on the first six but maximally strong on reviewability and revisability — which is why a *written, canonical, reviewable lexicon* is the correct grounding substrate for a swarm, where a human team would rely on talk.
- Costs trade off: as one grounding cost decreases another increases; parties shift to whichever method is cheapest *for the pair*. Documented consequences of failed common ground: **actor-observer misattribution, disappointment/eroded trust, and "multiple ignorances"** (undetected misunderstanding → decisions built on wrong shared representations). The last is the swarm failure mode respeak must guard against: two agents using the same shorthand token with silently divergent senses.

## 3. Zipf, the Brevity Law, and Piantadosi et al. 2011

### Zipf's principle of least effort
- Zipf, *Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort* (1949): "neither speakers nor hearers using a given language want to work any harder than necessary to reach understanding, and the process that results in approximately equal distribution of effort leads to the observed Zipf distribution" (Wikipedia, Zipf's law). Language structure is an equilibrium between speaker economy (few, short, ambiguous words) and hearer economy (many, distinct, explicit words). The principle was first articulated by Guillaume Ferrero (1894); Zipf systematized it.
- Zipf's rank-frequency law holds "for most natural languages, and even certain artificial ones such as Esperanto and Toki Pona" — so an artificial swarm dialect should be expected to develop the same skew, and its compression budget should be spent on the head of the frequency distribution.

### Zipf's law of abbreviation (brevity law), 1945
- "The more frequently a word is used, the shorter that word tends to be, and vice versa" (Wikipedia, Brevity law). Zipf showed the negative correlation for phoneme counts in American English and syllable counts in Latin.
- Since "empirically verified for almost a thousand languages of 80 different linguistic families"; holds acoustically (word duration), and even in nonhuman primate calls and whale vocalizations.
- Mechanism per the article: "a mediation between two major constraints: the pressure to reduce the cost of production and the pressure to maximize transmission success," explicitly linked to "principles of optimal data compression in information theory."
- Design consequence: assign the shortest codes to the *most frequent* swarm concepts — but only there. Rare concepts should stay long and self-describing (their expected savings are negligible and their ambiguity cost is high).

### Piantadosi, Tily & Gibson 2011 (PNAS 108(9):3526, doi:10.1073/pnas.1012551108)
Abstract (via Europe PMC; the pnas.org seed page redirects to the same DOI): "We demonstrate a substantial improvement on one of the most celebrated empirical laws in the study of language, Zipf's 75-y-old theory that word length is primarily determined by frequency of use. In accord with rational theories of communication, we show across 10 languages that average information content is a much better predictor of word length than frequency. This indicates that human lexicons are efficiently structured for communication by taking into account interword statistical dependencies. Lexical systems result from an optimization of communicative pressures, coding meanings efficiently given the complex statistics of natural language use."
- Refinement over Zipf: word length tracks **average surprisal in context** (n-gram information content), not raw frequency. Predictable-in-context words get short; words that carry high information when they occur stay long. This spreads information rate evenly across the utterance (uniform information density).
- Respeak consequence: the correct compression target is not "frequent phrase → short token" but "**low-surprisal-in-context phrase → short token**." Boilerplate that context already predicts (status framing, politeness, restatement) is the legitimate compression target; high-information content (identifiers, error specifics, numbers, novel decisions) should never be shortened, because short encodings of high-surprisal content are exactly where transmission errors become unrecoverable. This is the information-theoretic justification for a never_compress list.

## 4. Conventionalization: Experimental Semiotics

**Field definition** (Galantucci 2009, *Topics in Cognitive Science*; Galantucci & Garrod 2011 review): "the experimental investigation of novel forms of human communication" — lab games where people must bootstrap a communication system with no shared code, "reproducing micro samples of historical processes in the laboratory." (The Wikipedia seed page 404s; abstracts fetched via Europe PMC.)

### Galantucci 2005 coordination games (Cognitive Science 29:737–767)
Pairs played video games requiring coordination, exchanging marks through a medium that *prevented standard symbols* (a stylus pad that made drawn traces slide, so letters/pictures were impossible). Findings, verbatim from the abstract: "(a) signs originate from different mappings; (b) sign systems develop parsimoniously; (c) sign forms are perceptually distinct, easy to produce, and tolerant to variations."
- Also: "Communication systems emerged and developed rapidly during the games, integrating the use of explicit signs with information implicitly available to players and silent behavior-coordinating procedures." Systems co-opt *implicit context* — meaning leaks out of the visible channel into shared situational knowledge. For respeak: part of a swarm dialect's meaning will live in shared context that never appears on the wire, which is invisible to (and unrecoverable by) a human log-reader unless the lexicon records it.
- Success was far from universal: some pairs never converged (in the original study some pairs failed and quit). Convergence needs rapid, reliable feedback loops.
- Design criteria for good signs generalize directly to a token lexicon: **perceptually distinct** (no confusable near-neighbors), **easy to produce** (short), **tolerant to variation** (robust to paraphrase/typo).

### Garrod, Fay, Lee, Oberlander & MacLeod 2007, "Foundations of representation: where might graphical symbol systems come from?" (Cognitive Science 31:961–987)
The graphical "Pictionary" studies: partners repeatedly drew concepts (e.g., "Clint Eastwood," "theatre") for each other. Verbatim from the abstract:
- "Experiment 1 rules out a simple repetition based account in favor of an account that requires feedback and interaction between communicators." — icons do NOT simplify into symbols by mere repetition; **interaction/feedback is required** for conventionalization.
- "Experiment 2 shows how the degree of interaction affects the evolution of signs according to a process of grounding."
- "Experiment 3 confirms the prediction that **those not involved directly in the interaction have trouble interpreting the graphical signs produced**" — the overhearer problem. Drawings that started as recognizable icons degrade into minimal arbitrary squiggles perfectly legible to the pair and opaque to outsiders.
- Mechanism: "icons evolve into symbols as a consequence of the systematic shift in the locus of information **from the sign to the users' memory of the sign's usage**, supported by an interactive grounding process."
This is the single most important experimental result for respeak: as agents align, the meaning migrates out of the message and into the interaction history. The human reading the transcript is Experiment 3's outsider. A translator subagent is the countermeasure: it holds the interaction history the human lacks, and the governed lexicon is the externalized "memory of the sign's usage."

### Related result (Fay et al. community studies)
Fay, Garrod and colleagues extended the Pictionary paradigm to communities (members rotate partners): community-wide conventions emerge that are *more transparent/icon-like* than isolated-pair conventions, because each new pairing forces re-grounding toward forms interpretable without pair-specific history. Transferable principle: **forcing conventions to survive partner rotation keeps them more legible** — routing swarm messages through a translator (a perpetual "new partner") should measurably brake opacity drift.

## 5. Codified Expert Shorthands: Case Studies in Dictionary Governance

### 5.1 US Multi-Service Tactical Brevity Codes
- "Standardized procedure words used by multiple branches of the military to efficiently communicate complex information through concise, easily understood terms … intended to improve clarity, speed, and coordination in tactical operations" (Wikipedia). Governed as a joint Multi-Service Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures publication (ATP 1-02.1 / MCRP 3-30B.1 / NTTP 6-02.1 / AFTTP 3-2.5, maintained by the Air Land Sea Application Center and periodically re-ratified across services).
- **Dictionary structure worth copying:** every entry is TERM → single precise operational definition → **community/domain tags** ([A/A] air-to-air, [A/S], [EW], [MAR], [SO]) because "some codes may carry different meanings depending on the service or mission context," and those sense-splits are "noted in-line with each brevity code entry." Entries define oppositional pairs explicitly ("ALARM … Opposite of SNOOZE"; "AWAKE … Opposite of ASLEEP") and mandatory argument slots ("[number, weapon type] AWAY … At minimum, number or weapon type required").
- Some terms are only valid if pre-briefed: "[High or Low] ACCURACY: Prebriefed criteria is met … must be briefed by package commander (or outlined in the special instructions (SPINS))" — i.e., the global dictionary supports **mission-local parameterization declared up front**, respeak's per-session lexicon extensions.
- Examples of the compression ratio achieved: ANGELS = "height of friendly aircraft in thousands of feet MSL"; ARIZONA = "no antiradiation missile ordnance remaining"; AS FRAGGED = "unit will be performing exactly as briefed."
- Usage-restriction annotations: "ATTACK … Note: Not to be used in a close air support (CAS) engagement" — the dictionary bans a term in contexts where it is dangerous, not just defines it.

### 5.2 ICAO/ATC Standard Phraseology (safety-driven controlled vocabulary)
- Governing documents: **ICAO Annex 10 Vol II Ch. 5, Doc 4444 (PANS-ATM) Ch. 12, Doc 9432 Manual of Radiotelephony** (SKYbrary). ICAO amendments propagate to national authorities on notified timelines (e.g., Amendment 7-A to PANS-ATM, June 2016, harmonizing SID/STAR phraseology with a 12-month transition). A central body owns the phrasebook; nobody may locally extend it without creating a documented deviation.
- "Standard phraseology reduces the risk that a message will be misunderstood and aids the read-back/hear-back process so that any error is quickly detected. Ambiguous or non-standard phraseology is a frequent causal or contributory factor in aircraft accidents and incidents" (SKYbrary).
- Blood-priced precedent: miscommunication was central to the **1977 Tenerife disaster (583 dead)**, **Avianca 52 (1990, 73 dead — crew "failed to impart their critical fuel emergency")**, **Charkhi Dadri 1996 (349 dead)** (Wikipedia, Aviation English). The 2003 ICAO amendments made English proficiency (Operational Level 4) a licensing requirement.
- Structural features: mandatory **readback/hearback** loop (receiver must echo safety-critical items; sender must verify the echo); reserved metawords ("any correction of a misspoken word must always be conveyed using the word 'correction'"; "SAY AGAIN" for repeat); phonetic alphabet; number-pronunciation rules.
- Striking finding: **native English speakers are flagged as substandard communicators** — "prone to the use of non-standard terms, demonstrating impatience with non-native speakers, and speaking excessively, as well as too quickly. Such native speaker failings tend to worsen in emergency situations." Fluency is a liability when it breeds deviation from the controlled register. (Analogy: the most capable model is the most likely to drift stylistically.)
- Drift is documented even inside the system: SID/STAR phraseology benefits "have been eroded through the development of non-harmonised practices and different meanings being attached to certain elements" — requiring periodic central re-harmonization. Conventions decay without maintenance.

### 5.3 Ham Radio Q Codes
- Created c. 1909 by the British Postmaster General for ships and coast stations; 45 codes ratified in the **1912 International Radiotelegraph Convention**; current form standardized in the **ITU Radio Regulations, Atlantic City 1947**, and "most Q-codes defined in 1947 are still in use today" (Wikipedia). Governance = international treaty body with formal revision cycles.
- **Namespace allocation by governing authority:** QAA–QNZ reserved for aeronautical service (defined by ICAO), QOA–QQZ maritime, QRA–QUZ all services (allocated to the ITU), QVA–QZZ *deliberately unallocated* (reserved headroom). NATO militaries extend via a separate **Z code** rather than colliding with Q space ("ZBW 2" = change to backup frequency 2). Lesson: partition the code space by domain and reserve expansion room.
- **Deprecation on obsolescence:** QSW/QSX originally meant "Shall I increase/decrease my spark frequency?" — meaning retired when spark-gap transmitters were banned in the 1920s. Codes get re-pointed or retired by the central body, never informally.
- **Syntactic mode marking:** the same code is question with "?" and answer/imperative without ("QRP?" = "Shall I decrease transmitter power?"; "QRP" = "decrease your transmitter power") — one token, mode disambiguated by an explicit marker, not context.
- Aviation still uses QNH/QNE/QFE altimeter codes "as unambiguous shorthand, where safety and efficiency are of vital importance."

### 5.4 Maritime Signal Codes (International Code of Signals, INTERCO)
- 1857 Commercial Code: 18 flags generating "over 70,000 possible messages." **WWI stress-test verdict:** "when coding signals, word by word, the occasions upon which signaling failed were more numerous than those when the result was successful" — heavy general-purpose compression FAILED under operational stress.
- Governance history: 1932 Madrid Conference "set up a **standing committee for continual revision of the code**"; functions transferred to IMCO/IMO in 1959. The **1964 revision** narrowed scope: "a shift in focus from general communications to safety of navigation, **abandonment of the 'vocabulary' method of spelling out messages word by word**" — a mature system deliberately shrank its compression ambitions to the safety-critical core. Critical precedent: the right response to compression failure is scope reduction, not more codes.
- **Length allocated by urgency and frequency (Zipfian by design):** "Single-letter signals which are very urgent, important, or common. Two-letter signals for other messages … Three-letter signals beginning with 'M' [reserved namespace]: the Medical Signal Codes."
- **Translation layer built in:** "all of the standardized messages come in nine languages … each language has a book with equivalent messages keyed to the same code." The code IS the interlingua; human-facing renderings are per-audience projections of one canonical entry — exactly respeak's lexicon + translator architecture.
- Explicit in-band mode signal: naval ships fly the code pennant / say "Interco" to declare "I am now speaking ICS" — shorthand use is announced, never assumed.

### 5.5 Medical Abbreviations & The Joint Commission "Do Not Use" List (compression banned after harm)
- Timeline (from the official TJC document, fetched from web.archive.org): 2001 Sentinel Event Alert on medical abbreviations → 2002 Board-approved National Patient Safety Goal "requiring accredited organizations to develop and implement a list of abbreviations not to use" → **2004 official "Do Not Use" list** → 2010 integrated into Information Management standards (IM.02.02.01 EP 2–3).
- **The official list, verbatim (Do Not Use → Potential Problem → Use Instead):**
  - **U, u** (unit) → mistaken for "0", "4", or "cc" → write "unit"
  - **IU** → mistaken for IV or "10" → write "International Unit"
  - **Q.D./QD/q.d./qd and Q.O.D./QOD/q.o.d/qod** → mistaken for each other; period after Q mistaken for "I" → write "daily" / "every other day"
  - **Trailing zero (X.0 mg); lack of leading zero (.X mg)** → decimal point missed → write "X mg" / "0.X mg"
  - **MS, MSO4, MgSO4** → "can mean morphine sulfate or magnesium sulfate; confused for one another" → spell out.
- **Scoped exception, precisely bounded:** trailing zero "may be used only where required to demonstrate the level of precision of the value being reported (laboratory results, imaging …). It may not be used in medication orders." Ban rules carry context conditions, not blanket prohibitions.
- **Ratification process:** November 2004 National Summit on Medical Abbreviations — 70+ professional societies; conclusions posted for public comment; 5,227 responses / 15,485 comments in four weeks; ">80 percent of the respondents supported the creation and adoption of a 'do not use' list." A ban list was itself ratified by open comment — governance applied to *removals* as well as additions.
- The broader ISMP "List of Error-Prone Abbreviations, Symbols, and Dose Designations" extends the banned set; UK NICE/BNF goes further: "all prescriptions should be in English **without abbreviation** (apart from some units such as mg and mL; micrograms and nanograms should *not* be abbreviated)" (Wikipedia, List of abbreviations used in medical prescriptions).
- Failure taxonomy behind the bans (transferable to token design): (a) visual near-neighbor collision (U/0/4); (b) cross-code collision (IU/IV); (c) intra-family confusion of minimally different codes (QD/QOD); (d) fragile delimiters (decimal points); (e) one abbreviation, two referents (MS). Every one has a direct analogue in a token shorthand.

### 5.6 Court Stenography Brief Theory (Gregg/Pitman)
- Machine stenography "theories" are rule sets mapping sounds to strokes plus **brief forms** (abbreviations) for high-frequency words — pure applied brevity law: Gregg writes "can" as *k*, "please" as *pl* (Wikipedia, Gregg shorthand).
- **The personal-brief problem:** "Historically, reporters often created 'briefs' on-the-fly, and sometimes mixed theories, which could make it difficult for one reporter to read another reporter's notes." Modern CAT (computer-aided transcription) fixed this: theories "are primarily designed for computerized translation using a **standardized dictionary** provided by the company that promulgates the theory, which **forces reporters to stick with one theory** and use only the specific combinations in that company's dictionary" — though reporters still "add a significant number of entries to a stock dictionary" (personal extensions layered on a canonical base) (Wikipedia, Stenotype).
- **Gregg edition history = a century of tuning the brief-form count:** Pre-Anniversary 1916 (max briefs, max speed); Anniversary 1929 ("reduced the memory load on its learners by decreasing the number of brief forms"); Simplified 1949 (**181 briefs** — "drastically reduced," yet "one could still reach speeds upward of 150 WPM"); Diamond Jubilee 1963 (**129**); Series 90 1978 ("minimal number of brief forms and placed a great emphasis on clear transcription, rather than reporting speed"); Centennial 1988 (132). Empirical lesson: most of the speed survives aggressive dictionary shrinkage — the marginal brief buys little speed but costs learnability and transcription clarity. A respeak lexicon should be small; returns on entries diminish fast.
- The **scopist** role is a professional human translator of shorthand output: "trained in the phonetic writing system, English punctuation, and usually in legal formatting," turning raw steno into certified transcripts — an existing profession isomorphic to respeak's translator subagent (including the failure mode: "misstroked words may not translate and would appear in steno" — untranslatable tokens surface raw and must be flagged).

### 5.7 Open-Outcry Trading Hand Signals
- Floor hand signals (CME etc.) encode direction, price, quantity in a noisy channel: "Signals that occur with palms facing out and hands away from the body are an indication the gesturer wishes to sell. When traders face their palms in and hold their hands up, they are gesturing to buy" ; numbers 1–5 on the hand, 6–10 held sideways at 90°, blocks of ten from the forehead (Wikipedia, Open outcry).
- Governance is the weakest of all systems surveyed — largely community convention: "These rules may vary among exchanges or even among floors within the same exchange." Consequence: signals were exchange-local dialects with no portability, learned by apprenticeship only, and the whole medium died when a cheaper channel (electronic trading) arrived. Cautionary tale: an ungoverned shorthand is a stranded asset.
- Design positives: semantics bound to *orientation contrast* (palm in/out = buy/sell) gives maximal signal separation for the most critical bit — the highest-stakes distinction gets the most robust encoding.

## 6. Transferable Design Principles for respeak

1. **Count the reader's effort in the total.** Least *collaborative* effort includes the decoder. The human is a perpetual newcomer/overhearer (Garrod et al. 2007, Exp. 3: outsiders can't read pair-evolved signs), so every swarm message needs either a translation or a lexicon-resolvable form. Compression that strands the human violates the very principle that motivates it.
2. **Compression is inevitable; make it explicit.** Alignment/entrainment happens automatically (Pickering & Garrod; LLMs already shown to entrain). Convert implicit routinization into Clark-style negotiated pacts: a term enters the shorthand register only via an exposed-correction event — proposal, definition, ratification — recorded in the lexicon. Unratified shorthand in swarm output is a lint error.
3. **One canonical governed dictionary, one owner, versioned, with a revision cycle.** Every mature system converged on this: ITU for Q codes, ICAO for phraseology, IMO standing committee for ICS, ALSA for brevity codes, TJC for the ban list, theory vendors for steno dictionaries. Drift happens even inside governed systems (SID/STAR erosion) — schedule re-harmonization.
4. **Dictionary entry format** (from brevity codes): term → exact expansion → domain tags where senses differ → oppositional pair links → required argument slots → context bans ("not to be used in X") → ratification date/version. Support session-local extensions only if declared up front (SPINS pattern).
5. **Zipf/Piantadosi allocation:** shortest codes to highest-frequency, *lowest-surprisal-in-context* meanings (ICS: single letters for "very urgent, important, or common"). Never compress high-information content: identifiers, numbers, units, error specifics, novel decisions, negations.
6. **A never_compress list is a ratified artifact with a failure story per entry.** The TJC model: each ban records the confusion mode and the mandated replacement, allows precisely-scoped exceptions, and was itself adopted by open comment. Ban categories to import: visually/lexically confusable near-neighbor codes; one code with two referents; minimally-different code pairs (QD/QOD pattern); fragile delimiters; codes colliding with existing notation (IU/IV pattern).
7. **Sign quality criteria** (Galantucci 2005): distinct, easy to produce, tolerant to variation. Enforce a minimum edit distance between lexicon tokens; reject new tokens confusable with existing ones or with common English words/identifiers.
8. **Keep the dictionary small.** Gregg's century of revisions: 150 WPM survived cutting to 181 briefs; later editions traded speed for "clear transcription." Cap lexicon size; require usage-frequency evidence to retain entries; retire cold entries (Q-code deprecation pattern).
9. **Mode and register marking.** Declare shorthand use in-band (ICS code pennant / "Interco"); mark question vs. statement explicitly (Q-code "?"); reserve metawords for repair ("CORRECTION", "SAY AGAIN") that may never be compressed or repurposed.
10. **Readback for high-stakes messages.** ICAO's readback/hearback is grounding made mandatory: for irreversible or costly actions, the receiving agent must echo the decoded meaning and the sender must verify. Downstream correct action = cheap grounding evidence; echo = strong evidence.
11. **Partner rotation brakes opacity.** Community studies (Fay/Garrod): conventions that must survive new partners stay more transparent. Routing messages through the translator — and periodically having a fresh agent (or the human) decode a sample from the lexicon alone — keeps the dialect legible. If a term can't be decoded from its lexicon entry by a party without interaction history, the entry is inadequate (the meaning has migrated into "memory of usage" — re-ground it).
12. **When to decompress entirely:** the grounding literature's three triggers — time pressure on mutual understanding, elevated error probability/history, and notable ignorance gaps — plus the ICS precedent: after compression demonstrably fails (WWI word-by-word signaling), *shrink scope* to the safety-critical core rather than adding codes. Emergencies, escalations to the human, and anything touching the never_compress domains go in plain language (UK prescription rule: no abbreviations at all in the highest-stakes register).
13. **Fluency breeds deviation.** ICAO's finding that native speakers are the worst offenders (non-standard terms, excess speed, worse under stress) predicts the most capable agents will drift most; lint the strongest agents hardest, especially in incident/error paths.
14. **Exploit the text medium's strengths.** Of Clark & Brennan's eight media affordances, agent logs maximize *reviewability* and *revisability* — so unlike human talk, every pact can be permanently recorded, diffed, and audited. The lexicon file is the swarm's common ground made inspectable; treat divergence between usage and lexicon ("multiple ignorances") as a monitored failure condition.
